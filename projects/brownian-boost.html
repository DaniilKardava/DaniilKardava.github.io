Brownian Boost: incentives in motion.

A research project about a simple strategic tension: when two players can pay to influence a random contest, how does an imbalance in their incentives reshape the equilibrium?

Research context

How much imbalance can a fair-looking game hide?

Discrete gameOn the Trail of Lost Pennies ↗ Continuum limitFrom Tug-of-War to Brownian Boost ↗

This project builds on Alan Hammond’s On the Trail of Lost Pennies and From Tug-of-War to Brownian Boost.

A token moves back and forth on the integer lattice. Maxine wants to push it to the right; Mina wants to push it to the left. Both players are offered a reward if the ticker eventually drifts in their direction. At every position, both players choose a stake. Larger stakes improve their chance of controlling the next step, but they also cost more—so every move asks the same question: how much is a small advantage worth?

In the simplest version of the game, if Maxine and Mina stake a and b, the probability of moving right is

P(right) = a/(a + b)

Equilibrium strategies

One local incentive describes an entire strategy.

The newer model places the original move rule inside a two-parameter family:

P(right) = (1 − κ)/2 + κaρ/(aρ + bρ)

Keeping κ, ρ in (0,1)2, κ controls the mixture between a fair coin and competition, while ρ determines how strongly the contest rewards the larger stake.

We study strategies that depend only on the token’s position. It turns out that this entire class of equilibrium strategies can be parameterized by a single positive number, x: the ratio of Mina’s derivative of expected return to Maxine’s derivative of expected return near the center of the board. We call x the local incentive.

Finally, we define a function Mκ,ρ(x) on this variable. For each equilibrium strategy indexed by x, Mκ,ρ(x) records the asymmetry in the players’ incentives: how much Mina is offered upon winning for every dollar Maxine is offered in that equilibrium strategy. We are interested in the range of M across the entire family of equilibrium strategies.

Two directions

One object, two ways to study it.

The project combines computational experiments with a developing theoretical account of why the curves have the structure we observe.

Numerical track

A small signal hidden in a large series.

The formulas for M are explicit, but evaluating them is not straightforward. Each value is assembled from a long iterated series. In the most interesting regimes, many large contributions nearly cancel and leave a result extremely close to 1.

h(x; κ, ρ) = M(x; κ, ρ) − 1

Ordinary floating-point arithmetic can lose the tiny remainder that tells us which player has the stronger local incentive. As ρ becomes small, the series also requires more terms and the curve develops increasingly fine oscillations. The computational challenge is to preserve that structure without mistaking numerical noise for behavior of the model.

Techniques used

Explore quickly. Recompute carefully. Track the error.

Several numerical paths let the project trade speed for precision and then check delicate regions with stronger methods.

Double

Fast C++ sweeps reveal the broad geometry and identify regions that deserve closer inspection.

MPFR

Configurable-precision arithmetic preserves centered values that disappear in ordinary float64 calculations.

Arb

Interval arithmetic encloses results and makes numerical uncertainty visible instead of hiding it in a decimal.

Truncation

Tail bounds choose the series depth adaptively as the parameters move into more difficult regimes.

Safeguards

Explicit bracketing, centered evaluation, and endpoint handling prevent optimization failures from looking like valid answers.

Scale

OpenMP and cluster scripts distribute parameter sweeps while retaining the diagnostics needed to revisit each point.

What the computation reveals

From incentive maps to vibrating curves.

The same object can be viewed globally across parameters or locally as a curve. Together, these views show where asymmetric incentives are strongest and how the structure becomes more intricate near the edge of the parameter regime.

Theoretical track

Why does M have this shape?

The theoretical side will connect the geometry seen in the experiments to the underlying equilibrium equations: how asymmetry propagates through the game, why λmax forms bands in parameter space, and what controls the oscillatory small-ρ limit.
