How many ways can ships share a board without colliding?

A board arrangement is determined by choosing a position and orientation for every ship. Checking whether one arrangement is legal is easy: no two ships may occupy the same square. Counting every legal arrangement is much harder because the number of possibilities multiplies rapidly with the size of the board and fleet.

This project approaches that count indirectly. It temporarily allows ships to collide, assigns a penalty to every overlap, and gradually strengthens that penalty. Measurements taken along the way reveal how much of the original space remains once only collision-free boards are allowed.

The idea

Count the easy world, then measure the path to the hard one.

If overlaps are allowed, counting is straightforward: each labeled ship may independently occupy any position that fits on the board. Call this known total Nall. The answer we want, Nlegal, counts only the boards with no collisions.

Give every board x an energy E(x) that measures its overlap. Legal boards have zero energy; collisions produce positive energy. The partition function Z(β) is a weighted count of every board:

Z(β) = Σx exp(−β E(x))

The parameter β is a penalty dial. At β = 0 every board has weight one, so Z(0) = Nall. As β tends to infinity, every positive-energy board is suppressed while every zero-energy board keeps weight one, so Z(∞) = Nlegal.

The average energy under the distribution at β gives the rate at which this weighted count shrinks:

d log Z(β) / dβ = −𝔼β[E]

Integrating that rate from the unrestricted endpoint to the collision-free endpoint gives the desired count:

Nlegal = Nall exp(−∫0∞ 𝔼β[E] dβ)

In practice, the Gibbs sampler draws boards at a sequence of β values. Their energies estimate 𝔼β[E] at each point, and those estimates are used to approximate the integral numerically.

Efficient dynamic sampling

Every move asks a newly changed distribution for one sample.

A Gibbs update removes one ship and redraws its position conditioned on the rest of the board. That sounds like one random choice, but making it requires a probability for every possible placement—and those probabilities change again as soon as the board changes.

The sampling request

Turn the current board into one weighted placement.

After removing a ship, the sampler considers all M positions where that ship could fit. Placement i receives energy Ei according to how it overlaps the remaining fleet and weight exp(−βEi). The program needs one draw from those normalized weights before it can continue to the next Gibbs update.

The custom bucket sampler

Energy is integer-valued, so many placements share only a small number L of distinct weights. The sampler maintains a bucket Be containing every placement at energy e. Its probability mass is the number of members times their shared Boltzmann weight.

When a ship moves, only placements crossing its changed squares transfer between buckets. The maintained distribution is updated locally instead of reconstructed over all M placements.

02 · Full-size board, closed-form answer

Ten point ships on a 10×10 board.

When k = 1, a legal board assigns ten labeled ships to ten distinct cells. The exact answer no longer requires enumerating 10010 assignments: it is the falling factorial below. This makes the archived large run independently checkable.

Nlegal = 100 · 99 · … · 91 = 62,815,650,955,529,472,000

Sampled estimate: 6.281602×1019 legal arrangements. Relative error: 0.0006%.

03 · A compression conjecture

Can a few β values stand in for the whole curve?

The observed mean-energy curve is close to a single exponential in β. If that shape persists across larger problems, a small number of sampled β values could determine a and b.

Êβ = 0.4662 exp(−0.9996β)

These five evenly spaced points give an analytic integral of 0.46638, 0.30% above the exact 0.46497; the implied configuration count is within 0.14%. This is an encouraging proof of concept, not yet evidence that one exponential works for every grid, fleet, or energy definition.
